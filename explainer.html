Why a wider FOV makes a 360° panorama feel 3D

It is not stereoscopic depth — it is a perceptual trick from the camera's field of view.

Equirectangular projection · Perspective math · Peripheral vision

TL;DR

Your PNG is wrapped on the inside of a sphere; a virtual camera sits at the centre. The camera has a field of view in degrees. A narrow FOV is a telephoto lens — it crops a flat-looking slice. A wide FOV approaches the angular range of a human eye, so the curvature of the sphere becomes visible at the edges and your brain reads it as "being inside a place" rather than "looking at a photo."

1 The source image is a sphere, unrolled

The PNG you loaded is an equirectangular projection. Every column of pixels maps to a longitude (0–360°) and every row to a latitude (–90° to +90°), exactly like a Mercator world map. That is why the aspect ratio is 2:1 and why the very top and bottom of the image look stretched: the two poles of the sphere are smeared across the full image width.

Check: any 360° panorama needs a 2:1 aspect ratio (2000×1000, 4096×2048, 8192×4096…). If it is not 2:1, it is not a full sphere and the viewer will distort it.

2 The viewer flips a sphere inside-out

Three.js (which Photo Sphere Viewer wraps) builds a sphere mesh, applies the panorama as a texture, and inverts the normals so the textured side faces inward. The camera is placed at the centre. From in there, every direction you look hits the texture — there is no "outside" of the world.

3 FOV is the whole trick

A perspective camera projects the slice of the world inside a cone onto your flat screen. The cone's opening angle is the field of view. Photo Sphere Viewer exposes it as minFov / maxFov in degrees, and what users call "zoom" is just changing this angle.

4 Why wide reads as "3D"

There are three perceptual cues stacking up, and none of them are real depth:

Peripheral fill. A human eye covers roughly 120° horizontally. At 100° FOV the panorama fills almost all of your screen-mediated peripheral vision, so there is no flat edge to anchor your "this is a picture" reading.
Edge curvature. Straight lines in the world (the tree trunks, the bridge edge) bend slightly at the frame boundary because a wide perspective projection has to fan a large angular range onto a flat rectangle. That bending is the same cue you get from a wide-angle camera lens, and your brain has learned to read it as "I am inside this space."
Motion parallax that matches head-turn. When you drag, the view rotates around your eye-point. At wide FOV, the speed at which features sweep across the frame matches what they would do if you actually turned your head in a real environment. At narrow FOV they slide unnaturally, like panning a poster.

5 Why narrow felt flat

A 30° FOV slice of an equirectangular panorama is geometrically nearly identical to a flat printout of that crop. The sphere is so big relative to the cone that the visible patch is almost a plane. No curvature, no peripheral fill, and rotating just slides a 2D picture sideways. So your brain — correctly — calls it 2D.

Reality check. None of this is stereoscopic 3D. There is no real depth in the image — both your eyes see the same pixels. The "3D feeling" is entirely a side-effect of FOV, peripheral fill, and motion. True depth would require either a depth map plus reprojection, or a stereo pair (one panorama per eye, as in VR).

6 The settings we landed on

new Viewer({ panorama: '…2:1 equirectangular png…', minFov: 30, // floor — anything below feels overzoomed maxFov: 110, // ceiling — beyond ~120° edges warp painfully defaultZoomLvl: 15 // drop user near the wide end on load });

If you want it even more dramatic, push maxFov to 120 — that is around the limit before the equirectangular-to-perspective remap starts visibly bending vertical lines into curves. Below 60 the scene flattens out and the spell breaks; that is why ChatGPT's viewer never lets you zoom out further than it does either.

One caveat. Source resolution caps the wide-end feel. A 2000×1000 image at FOV 100° spreads roughly 555 px across your screen's horizontal — fine, but a 4096×2048 source will look noticeably sharper at the same FOV and the immersion holds up better.
